Torpedo fire control: the arithmetic of an attack

Every attack data sheet in a war patrol report ends in a table of numbers: Track 123S, Gyro 013, Depth Set 6. They are the answer to a triangle: where the target would be when a torpedo, fired now, reached its track. This page explains the terms as the Navy defined them, works one of BANG's attacks from her own sheet, and lets you set up the triangle yourself.

The gyro: aiming the torpedo, not the boat

A submarine did not have to point at its target. The Navy's doctrine defines the gyro angle as "an angle applied in the mechanism of a torpedo gyro before firing which causes the torpedo, immediately upon launching, to turn through an angle and steady on a course differing from the direction of the torpedo tube axis". It is measured from the boat's bow, "from 0° clockwise through 360°": a straight shot from a bow tube is 0°, a straight shot from a stern tube 180°, a 90° right-angle shot from a bow tube 90° and from a stern tube 270° (USF 25(A) p. 37). So the torpedo's course is the boat's course plus the gyro angle.

The turn is not instant. The torpedo runs out of the tube and swings onto its gyro course along a curve, so its final track is offset from the tube: the doctrine calls the sideways part of that offset the advance, and applies a "correction for advance and transfer" to angled shots, and a correction for parallax for the distance between the tube and the periscope (USF 25(A) pp. 37–38). It also dives: "As the torpedo is launched, its initial dive takes it below its pre-set depth. The depth mechanism brings it back to set depth" (OP 635 Ch. 1, pp. 10–13), which is why the doctrine wants a run of no less than 500 yards, so the torpedo "will settle to its set depth" (USF 25(A) p. 50).

The limits were the mechanism's. The Mark 14-1A's gyro allowed "angle shots up to 90 degrees right or left"; the Mark 14-3A's "up to 160 degrees". The Mark 14-1A and 14-3A had two speeds, "31 and 46 knots, having a range of 9,000 yards and 4,500 yards respectively"; the Mark 23 was a 14-3A locked in high power, "operated at high speed only" (OP 635 pp. 13–14). The doctrine preferred small gyro angles: "The best chances of hitting are with small gyro angles", and "the straight shot is the better shot when feasible" (USF 25(A) pp. 44, 52).

The problem the Torpedo Data Computer solved

The approach officer could measure three things directly: the target's bearing (periscope, radar or sound), its range (stadimeter, radar or echo ranging) and, by eye from its aspect, its angle on the bow: the "relative bearing of submarine from the target ship measured to starboard or port from the target ship's head from 0° to 180°" (USF 25(A) pp. 37, 49). From a series of these the target's course and speed were worked out. The Torpedo Data Computer Mark 3 did that work in two parts: a position keeper, which kept the target's position moving on its estimated course and speed, and an angle solver, which worked the shot (OP 1056, Section 1: the position keeper's equations p. 22, the angle solver's p. 29). "At the beginning of the war most submarines carried the Torpedo Data Computer Mark 3"; the Mark 4 followed, for radar data the Mark 3 "could be only partially utilized" with and for the Mark 18 torpedo (Rowland and Boyd pp. 399-400). These pages take BANG's to have been a Mark 3 (assumed: no record read names her computer's mark; the Mark 3's manual was issued to boats "SS 167-544 incl." in June 1944, OP 1056 p. 277). "The gyro angle, track angle and torpedo run are continuously being computed … The computed gyro angle is set on the torpedo continuously either automatically or by follow the pointer, spread angles being superimposed as desired" (USF 25(A) p. 50).

The answer is a triangle: the boat, the target at the moment of firing, and the intercept point, "the intersection of torpedo and target tracks" (USF 25(A) p. 38). Its angles are:

  • at the target, the angle on the bow;
  • at the boat, the lead angle: how far ahead of the target the torpedo is aimed (the doctrine's "sight angle", "the angle between the bearing of the target at the instant of firing and the torpedo track", USF 25(A) p. 37);
  • at the intercept, the supplement of the track angle: "the angle at the point of intercept between the target ship's track and the reversed direction of the torpedo track … measured to starboard or to port from the target ship's head from 0° to 180°" (USF 25(A) p. 37). So track angle = angle on the bow + lead angle.

The torpedo and the target reach the intercept together, so their runs are in the ratio of their speeds, and the lead angle follows from the target's speed against the torpedo's (the law of sines). That trigonometry is ours, worked from the definitions above, and is the kit's computation in site/engage.js (fireControl, attackSheet), which the figure and the worked example below both call. It leaves out the advance and transfer and the parallax, so on an angled shot it is a degree or two and some tens of yards from what the computer set (inferred).

The equations

The same triangle, written out. These are worked from the doctrine's definitions above, and they are the equations engage.js computes; the Navy's own working, in the computer, is in OP 1056's section on the angle solver, which is not online in full. The symbols:

Co,Ct,Cτ
the courses of own boat, the target and the torpedo, in degrees true;
Vt,Vτ
the target's and the torpedo's speeds, in knots, and k=VtVτ their ratio;
R
the range at firing; β the target's relative bearing (from own bow);
AOB,L,TA
the angle on the bow, the lead angle and the track angle; G the gyro angle.

1. Where the torpedo goes. Its course is own course plus the gyro angle, and the track angle is the angle between the target's head and the torpedo coming at it (starboard when it is positive):

Cτ=Co+G TA=Cτ+180°−Ct

2. The triangle. Its angles add up to 180°: the lead at the boat, the angle on the bow at the target, and 180° less the track angle at the intercept. So

TA=AOB+L

3. Arriving together. In the time t the torpedo takes to reach the intercept, the torpedo runs Vτt and the target Vtt. Each side of the triangle is opposite one angle, so by the law of sines

Vttsin⁡L=Vτtsin⁡AOB=Rsin⁡TA

and the lead angle follows from the angle on the bow and the two speeds alone (the range drops out):

sin⁡L=ksin⁡AOB

or, from the track angle instead, which is how fireControl works it from a sheet's typed track:

tan⁡L=ksin⁡TA1+kcos⁡TA

4. The gyro angle. The torpedo is aimed ahead of the target by the lead: to the right of its bearing when the target shows its starboard side (it is crossing to the right), to the left when it shows its port side:

G=β±L

5. The runs. From the law of sines again, the torpedo's run, the time it takes and the target's run to the intercept:

Dτ=Rsin⁡AOBsin⁡TA,t=DτVτ,Dt=Rsin⁡Lsin⁡TA

with a knot taken as 2,025 yards an hour (a nautical mile), so a 46-knot torpedo runs about 26 yards a second. The figure below shows the numbers going in, for whatever you set.

Try it: with a 10-knot target at 60° starboard, the torpedo leads by about 11°. Raise the angle on the bow to 90° and the lead is greatest for that speed. Swing the angle towards 0° (the target coming straight at you) or 180° (going away) and the lead shrinks, but the track angle goes to 0° or 180°, which the doctrine counts poor shots: "Track angles between 70° and 110° are considered best for all around coverage" (USF 25(A) p. 53).

If the boat is moving

She usually was: the doctrine has the Torpedo Data Computer kept ready with "own course and speed automatic" (USF 25(A) p. 59), the submarine's speed being "the speed through the water as determined by underwater log" (p. 38); in the computer, "The speed and course of the submarine were automatically fed into the mechanical unit; data on the enemy's position and movement was manually inserted" (Rowland and Boyd p. 399). Her motion matters in three places, and the triangle above is not one of them (the reasoning here is ours, from the definitions):

  • Not at the moment of firing. Once it is running, the torpedo goes through the water at its own speed on its own course, wherever the boat goes next. The triangle is between the torpedo, from where it was launched, and the target, so own speed does not appear in it. Set an own speed in the figure: the boat moves on (dotted) while the torpedo runs, and the gyro angle, lead and run do not change.
  • Before firing: the position keeper. Bearing and range change because both ships move. The position keeper ran the target's position forward from the last observation using both ships' courses and speeds. In a frame fixed on own boat, the target moves at the difference of the two velocities: dxdt=Vtsin⁡Ct−Vosin⁡Co,dydt=Vtcos⁡Ct−Vocos⁡Co R=x2+y2,true bearing=atan2(x,y),β=true bearing−Co (east and north, in yards). The bearings it produced this way were the doctrine's "generated bearings", which a boat could fire on between looks, "from recent periscope observations" (USF 25(A) p. 52). If own course or speed was wrong, so were they (inferred). The computer's own equations are on OP 1056 p. 22, not yet read.
  • Between the torpedoes of a spread. The boat keeps moving between shots, so each torpedo starts a little farther on: at 3 knots she makes about 1.7 yards a second, some 14 yards between torpedoes fired 8 seconds apart. The worked example below places each torpedo where the boat was when it was fired, from the sheet's own course and speed.
  • Turning to fire. The doctrine also obtains a divergent spread "by swinging the submarine" (p. 53): the boat turns between shots and the gyro angles stay put, so the torpedoes' courses fan out with her heading.

Reading an attack data sheet

Each attack in a war patrol report has a two-page data sheet. The first page gives the time and position, the target's description, draft, course, speed and range, the boat's own speed, course and depth, and the type of attack in words; the second has one column per torpedo (SEAHORSE's sheets, rg38-74845019/p29–30; BANG's, rg38-74802746/p16–17). The kit transcribes the numbers, as typed, into each patrol's attacks.csv (one row a torpedo) and the words into attack_sheets.yml. The fields, and how they check each other:

Tubes Fired
Tubes 1–6 are a fleet boat's bow tubes and 7–10 its stern tubes (BANG's stern shots are typed with gyros near 180°, tubes 7–10, rg38-74802746/p21).
Track Angle
With S or P, the side of the target the torpedo meets. Check: own course + gyro angle + 180° − target course should give it.
Gyro Angle
From the bow, clockwise (USF 25(A) p. 37). Some boats type right or left of the bow (15R, 5L).
Depth Set
Feet. The doctrine set 6 feet for destroyers and 10–12 for merchant ships when using the older exploders, and "When using the Mark 6 exploder mechanism set torpedo depth in accordance with the latest instructions from the Force Commander" (USF 25(A) pp. 51–52).
Power
High or low, the two-speed Mark 14's setting (OP 635 pp. 13–14); doctrine was "the highest torpedo speed which will reach the target" (USF 25(A) p. 50). BANG's sheets leave it blank (−) for the single-speed Mark 23s.
Firing Interval and Type Spread
Seconds between torpedoes, and how they were spread: BANG's first sheets type each torpedo's offset from the point of aim (1½R, ½L), applied "by means of the offset knob on TDC" (rg38-74802746/p16).
Hit or Miss, Erratic
As the boat judged them, from what was seen and heard and the timed explosions: a claim, which the other side's records test.

Tactics of the day

The submerged approach. The 1944 doctrine breaks an attack into getting ahead of the enemy unseen, closing submerged, firing on a good track, and getting away. Periscope looks were short ("Two or more brief exposures of less than 30 seconds are preferable to a long exposure"), and it favoured "a driving in approach where the angle on the bow is small" (USF 25(A) pp. 41–42, 46). It assumed discovery: "discovery at the time of firing torpedoes is almost inevitable", so the boat retires at once and reloads (pp. 56, 62).

The night surface attack. "Remain on the surface, in order to maintain the advantages of mobility, until there is a possibility of detection. When detection is possible, go to radar depth"; "Submarines have been sighted on bright moonlight nights at 12,000 yards range" (USF 25(A) p. 59). BANG's Attack 2, the next morning, was a "Night surface radar attack, using constant radar ranges and constant TBT bearings to TDC" (rg38-74802746/p19): the bearings from the target bearing transmitter on the bridge, the ranges from radar.

Spreads. "Spreads are always used. The minimum spread should cover at least 80% of the target length", and up to 200% when the problem was less well solved. A longitudinal spread fires along one track at different points of aim, "the simplest to apply … used principally with periscope firing"; a divergent spread fans the torpedoes with different gyro angles at one point of aim, "more difficult to evade"; a parallel spread from bow and stern tubes together was "undesirable" (USF 25(A) pp. 37–38, 53). BANG's offsets of 1½R to 1½L on Attack 1 are a spread of the first kind, set on the computer.

Bow and stern shots, and the escorts. If the target turned at the last minute, the doctrine changed the gyro angle or shifted "from bow to stern fire (or vice versa) rather than" turning the slow boat (USF 25(A) p. 45). "Offensive action should be taken whenever practicable against escorts of such a size as to warrant torpedo fire" (p. 56). The doctrine read here does not name the bow-on shot at an oncoming escort that later accounts call "down the throat"; we have not found it in a wartime source yet.

The torpedo failures of 1942–43. A secondary account by the Naval Undersea Museum: warshot torpedoes "ran deeper than the set depth because the warheads were heavier than exercise heads", corrected by "new depth calibrations"; the Mark 6 magnetic exploder "was detonating prematurely", so "the submarine force switched to the contact exploder"; and then a direct hit "would bend the firing pin", fixed with "a lighter firing pin and a heavier firing pin spring", so that "by fall 1943 … the MK 14 was finally functioning satisfactorily" (Ryan, Naval Undersea Museum, 2016). The wartime manuals show traces of it: the Mark 14-3's "depth mechanism … was improved, and the torpedo was designated the Torpedo Mk 14-3A" (OP 635 p. 13), and the February 1944 doctrine leaves the Mark 6's depth setting to "the latest instructions from the Force Commander" (USF 25(A) p. 52). BANG's sheets of 1944 set 6 to 8 feet (attacks.csv, patrol 1).

Wolf packs. The February 1944 doctrine says of coordinated attack only "These tactics are at present undergoing revision" (USF 25(A) p. 61). The packs' practice shows in the patrol reports: BANG, PARCHE and TINOSA in April–May 1944 sent each other contact reports, were asked for "positions of all submarines", and were given flanks to attack from (the Luzon Strait engagement, its notes from the three boats' reports).
