Reading the records / Depth charges

Depth charges: what a counter-attack was, in the water

A war patrol report's depth log says how deep the boat was, how many charges she counted and how close they sounded: "about 80 charges in patterns of 3 6 or 9", "directly above", "two depth charges set shallow exploded directly over head as we passed 300 ft." This page sets those words in the water: the charges the Japanese escorts carried and the depths their pistols could be set for, the shock wave and what the surface and the seafloor did to it, the sea's own weight on the hull, and what the Bureau of Ships concluded after the war about distance and damage.

The charges and their settings

Two types were in general use by the Japanese escorts, "both … almost exact copies of early British models of obsolete design": the Type 95 and the Type 2, adopted in 1942 (War Damage Report 58, paragraph 3-9). Each was a drum about 17½ inches across and 30½ inches long, filled as follows:

The pistol fired when sea water, let in through a small hole, filled an inner cylinder: "the smaller the inlet, the deeper the setting", and no other depths could be chosen (3-10). The deepest the Type 95 could be set "is believed to have been 292 feet and the maximum setting normally used for the Type 2 charge was 390 feet"; boats "reported only a few cases where depth charges were set for depth in excess of 400 feet" (3-12). How far a charge went off from its setting is not known: "No data are available" (3-11). A kaibokan "could carry as many as 300 and [was] usually fitted with twelve single depth charge throwers, six to a side, and one stern rack"; a fleet destroyer carried about 30 (3-8). The Bureau's verdict: "The Japanese failed to achieve optimum use of their principal anti-submarine weapon, the depth charge, simply by repeatedly setting their pistols too shallow" (3-22).

The settings against a boat's depth

A Balao-class boat to scale beside the settings. Move her down: the deeper she went, the fewer settings could reach her. Each charge is drawn straight above her, which is the worst case; where any one charge went off is never recorded.

The rings: the Bureau's distances for 300 lb of TNT against the older, lighter-hulled boats (SS-284 and earlier): "almost certainly … pressure hull rupture and therefore presumed lethal damage" within 25 ft; "rupture or marked permanent deformation … and/or serious damage to vital machinery" between 25 and 50; "moderate to minor damage to equipment and fittings … from 50 to 100 feet and beyond" (15-12). The Balao's heavier hull (SS-285 on) was stronger, so the rings are the scale of the danger, not her limits. Peak pressures: computed for the charge's weight taken as TNT (assumed), Cole's similitude, below.

The shock wave, the surface and the seafloor

A charge's explosion sends a pressure wave out at the speed of sound in sea water, about 4,900 feet a second, so it crosses a hundred feet in a fiftieth of a second. Where it meets the surface it comes back as a tension wave, which can "very materially diminish the main pressure pulse" at a target near a shallow detonation; where it meets a hard bottom it comes back as pressure, and "will theoretically be doubled for charges which are detonated on a perfectly rigid surface", which "would appear to operate in favor of anti-submarine forces attacking a target running" close over it (War Damage Report 58, section XV). Drawn: the direct wave (solid), the surface's reflection (dashed: a tension wave, which water can carry only so far before it cavitates), the seafloor's (dotted), each from its mirror image of the charge, as the ray paths go. Arrival times are distance over 4,900 ft/s (ours). The seafloor's strength depends on what it is, rock or sand or mud; the Bureau gives the upper limit (doubled, on a perfectly rigid surface), not a value.

How fast the pressure falls off

The peak pressure of the shock wave falls a little faster than the distance grows. The standard similitude for TNT (R. H. Cole, Underwater Explosions, 1948), in his units: P = 21,600 × (W1/3 / R)1.13 pounds per square inch, for W pounds at R feet. The Japanese explosives were not TNT; taking them as TNT is an assumption. Against it, the sea's own pressure on the hull: about 0.445 psi for every foot of depth, 200 psi at 450 ft.

Computed. The shaded bands are the Bureau's distances for 300 lb of TNT against a light hull, for comparison: within 25 ft, 25 to 50, 50 to 100. Peak pressure alone does not decide damage: how long the pulse lasts, the gas bubble's later pulses (worst when "the charge is detonated below the hull"), where it strikes and the boat's own depth all count. The Bureau itself warned that "present experimental and theoretical knowledge is still not sufficient to compute accurate damage versus distance relationships" (15-11).

Deeper was safer from the settings, and more dangerous at any one distance

The sea's weight is already on the hull before a charge goes off, and the shock adds to it. In model tests of the SS-285 class, "the lethal distance of a given weight of explosive was observed to increase rapidly with depth": the distance at which the model cylinder collapsed "was two and one-half times greater at 673 feet than at 200 feet" (15-14). Close charges also moved the boat: BALAO was "forced down from 390 foot depth to 590 foot depth by a series of close depth charge detonations, each … causing the boat to increase depth by about 20 or 30 feet" (section XV). The gas bubble that follows the shock reaches a radius of about 20 to 25 ft at periscope depth for an average charge of 300 to 600 lb, and about 12 to 15 ft at 300 ft (15-20).

Patterns, and what a log can say

The escorts dropped charges from the stern rack and fired them from throwers to either side, in patterns; "a proportionate number of charges with these maximum settings were usually included in each attack pattern" (3-12). BANG's report of 19 September 1944 gives the patterns as the boat heard them, "patterns of 3 6 or 9"; no Japanese record found gives the pattern a ship laid. A log can give the count, the time, the boat's depth and how the charges sounded and shook her; it cannot give where they went off. The Bureau quotes the boats' own words: "first a detonator click … and then bang!"; "a deep rumble increasing to the final boom"; "two pings on the hull which sounded as though two small nuts had been dropped" (section XV), and notes that the pistols had no mechanism to make a click. Damage says more than sound: a boat that came through undamaged was outside the lethal distance of every charge, and the damage she did take shows how near the nearest came.

In the records: the water column of BANG's counter-attack of 19 September 1944, and every engagement whose boat's depth log is transcribed; BANG's dives through every patrol.
