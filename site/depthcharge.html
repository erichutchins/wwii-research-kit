Reading the records / Depth charges

Depth charges: what a counter-attack was, in the water

A war patrol report's depth log says how deep the boat was, how many charges she counted and how close they sounded: "about 80 charges in patterns of 3 6 or 9", "directly above", "two depth charges set shallow exploded directly over head as we passed 300 ft." This page sets those words in the water: the charges the Japanese escorts carried and the depths their pistols could be set for, the shock wave and what the surface and the seafloor did to it, the sea's own weight on the hull, and what the Bureau of Ships concluded after the war about distance and damage.

The charges and their settings

Two types were in general use by the Japanese escorts, "both … almost exact copies of early British models of obsolete design": the Type 95 and the Type 2, adopted in 1942 (War Damage Report 58, paragraph 3-9). Each was a drum about 17½ inches across and 30½ inches long, filled as follows:

The pistol fired when sea water, let in through a small hole, filled an inner cylinder: "the smaller the inlet, the deeper the setting", and no other depths could be chosen (3-10). The deepest the Type 95 could be set "is believed to have been 292 feet and the maximum setting normally used for the Type 2 charge was 390 feet"; boats "reported only a few cases where depth charges were set for depth in excess of 400 feet" (3-12). How far a charge went off from its setting is not known: "No data are available" (3-11). A kaibokan "could carry as many as 300 and [was] usually fitted with twelve single depth charge throwers, six to a side, and one stern rack"; a fleet destroyer carried about 30 (3-8). The Japanese officers questioned by the Naval Technical Mission in 1945 put it the same way: "Maximum depth setting of charges usually carried was 120 meters [394 ft] and a proportionate number of charges with this setting was included in each pattern" (S-24, p. 12). The Bureau's verdict: "The Japanese failed to achieve optimum use of their principal anti-submarine weapon, the depth charge, simply by repeatedly setting their pistols too shallow" (3-22).

How an escort attacked

The escorts' doctrine, as their officers described it to the Naval Technical Mission after the war (report S-24, pp. 11-15). On an echo the escort reported the contact, turned toward it at twelve knots and kept a hand plot of the bearings; "At 800 meters increase speed to 16 knots and lead target by amount that hand plot indicated for depth charges to reach submarine", and dropped "based on firing time from chemical recorder". The other way was to slow to six or eight knots inside 800 meters, stop echo-ranging, and run down the propeller noises, dropping "when intensity of sound indicated the escort to be over the submarine target". Then: "The Japanese had no means of determining the depth of enemy submarines, and their depth charge patterns were designed to cover all depths within the limitations of the depth settings on their charges. Side throwers were used. Small patterns were used on doubtful contacts. Doctrine called for maximum patterns to be dropped on contacts classified definitely as submarines. Destroyers engaged in convoy work were capable of carrying ninety depth charges and on sure contacts dropped a pattern of thirty-six." The pattern's width came from the side throwers; "The barrage was completed by dropping the remainder of the charges from the stern racks."

Above, a kaibokan's pattern as S-24's figure 5 draws it from above: nineteen charges, each marked with its setting in meters (30, 60, 90, 120); the figure gives no distances, so it is drawn as it is drawn, not to scale. Below, a destroyer's pattern in depth after its figure 4, the charges 50 meters apart as the figure marks between the first two of a row (the same spacing assumed along the rest), the settings converted to feet, beside a BALAO class boat to scale at the depth you set: where a pattern could reach her, and where it could not.

After the attack: the search, and the next run

"The doctrine for regaining contact, after delivering an attack, required the escort to continue on the attack course for approximately 1000 meters, go to 12 knots and reverse course to the right, returning to the area of attack and skirting the turbulent area at a distance of about 500 meters, searching by echo-ranging, and, if contact were not regained after circumnavigating the turbulent area, to steam back through the original point of contact, stop and listen." Failing that, a single ship steamed an expanding spiral, "predicated on a submarine target speed of 3 knots"; convoy commanders "usually limited this type of search to twenty-four hours" (S-24, p. 13). Two or more escorts took turns: the senior attacked first while the others listened, ran on 600 or 700 meters, turned out, "stopped, discontinued echo-ranging, and listened", and the next began his run (p. 15). A boat's log hears this as patterns minutes apart for hours: BANG's "very deliberate depth charging … about 80 charges in patterns of 3 6 or 9 during the next 4½ hours", 19 September 1944.

How a kill was judged

The Grand Escort Fleet's board judged an escort's claim by "1. Oil and the length of time it continued to come up. 2. Debris 3. Insulating material 4. Buoys from submarines 5. Crew members captured" (S-24, p. 45), and allowed for its own crews' optimism: "Members of the board felt that their own operating personnel exaggerated claims and arrived at a 33% reduction factor to offset this." The escorts and aircraft that hunted BANG on 19 September reported her "certainly sunk" on oil; she surfaced that night undamaged (the counter-attack).

The shock wave, the surface and the seafloor

A charge's explosion sends a pressure wave out at the speed of sound in sea water, about 4,900 feet a second, so it crosses a hundred feet in a fiftieth of a second. Where it meets the surface it comes back as a tension wave, which can "very materially diminish the main pressure pulse" at a target near a shallow detonation; where it meets a hard bottom it comes back as pressure, and "will theoretically be doubled for charges which are detonated on a perfectly rigid surface", which "would appear to operate in favor of anti-submarine forces attacking a target running" close over it (War Damage Report 58, section XV). Drawn: the direct wave (solid), the surface's reflection (dashed: a tension wave, which water can carry only so far before it cavitates), the seafloor's (dotted), each from its mirror image of the charge, as the ray paths go. Arrival times are distance over 4,900 ft/s (ours). The seafloor's strength depends on what it is, rock or sand or mud; the Bureau gives the upper limit (doubled, on a perfectly rigid surface), not a value.

How fast the pressure falls off

The peak pressure of the shock wave falls a little faster than the distance grows. The standard similitude for TNT (R. H. Cole, Underwater Explosions, 1948), in his units: P = 21,600 × (W1/3 / R)1.13 pounds per square inch, for W pounds at R feet. The Japanese explosives were not TNT; taking them as TNT is an assumption. Against it, the sea's own pressure on the hull: about 0.445 psi for every foot of depth, 200 psi at 450 ft.

Computed. The shaded bands are the Bureau's distances for 300 lb of TNT against a light hull, for comparison: within 25 ft, 25 to 50, 50 to 100. Peak pressure alone does not decide damage: how long the pulse lasts, the gas bubble's later pulses (worst when "the charge is detonated below the hull"), where it strikes and the boat's own depth all count. The Bureau itself warned that "present experimental and theoretical knowledge is still not sufficient to compute accurate damage versus distance relationships" (15-11).

The hull

"In the hope of attaining added safety during attack, and also to better avoid detection, the pressure hull of the SS285 Class was designed to permit a much greater depth of submergence than that of any previous submarines in the U.S. Navy", with thicker plating of high tensile steel; "only two submarines of this Class reported sustaining permanent pressure hull deformation as the result of underwater explosive attack, although many were subjected to close detonations", and none any failure from going too deep (War Damage Report 58, 16-9). The Bureau's figure 15-2 gives the distances, both "for shallow submergence condition only": the Bureau's 1943 curve for deformation of the SS-212 class's 11/16-inch medium steel hull, and the Operations Evaluation Group's for rupture of the SS-285 class's 7/8-inch high tensile steel hull. "The data upon which this curve is based are sparse and incomplete" (15-13).

Read off the plate at every 100 lb, about ± 2 ft (ours); the Bureau's curve is the middle of its shaded band. The rules mark the Japanese charges: the Type 95 Mod. 0's 220 lb and the Type 2 Mod. 1's 357 lb, taken as TNT. At 357 lb: about 23 ft for an SS-285 class hull, about 44 ft for an SS-212's.

Deeper was safer from the settings, and more dangerous at any one distance

The sea's weight is already on the hull before a charge goes off, and the shock adds to it. In model tests of the SS-285 class, "the lethal distance of a given weight of explosive was observed to increase rapidly with depth": the distance at which the model cylinder collapsed "was two and one-half times greater at 673 feet than at 200 feet" (15-14). Close charges also moved the boat: BALAO was "forced down from 390 foot depth to 590 foot depth by a series of close depth charge detonations, each … causing the boat to increase depth by about 20 or 30 feet" (section XV). The gas bubble that follows the shock reaches a radius of about 20 to 25 ft at periscope depth for an average charge of 300 to 600 lb, and about 12 to 15 ft at 300 ft (15-20).

What the boats' damage looked like

War Damage Report 58's Appendix I briefs every case of damage the boats reported. Four from the waters and the boats of these records:

  • PICUDA, 20 May 1944, Luzon Strait: "Received three close charges while at 70 foot depth … Picuda went deep, receiving 4 more close charges at 150 foot depth, 4 at 250 foot depth and 3 at 450 foot depth" (p. 278): the settings followed her down.
  • APOGON, 12 July 1944, Luzon Strait: rammed at "about 70 feet to keel" by a freighter of the convoy she was attacking; "Several depth charges were dropped as Apogon went deep but did no damage" (p. 280; MOMA-01).
  • SEAHORSE, 18 April 1945, Tsushima Strait: "First pattern of 8 or 9 charges was received close aboard and forced boat from 300 foot to 400 foot depth. Propulsion was then secured and Seahorse settled on bottom in 470 feet of water. About 40 more charges were dropped but were not close" (p. 292).
  • PARCHE, 26 June 1945, off northern Honshu: "16 close charges detonated below ship while running at about 300 foot depth"; a sanitary tank's vent valve jarred open and flooded, and the boat went to 550 ft (p. 294). Below the hull is the worse side: the bubble's later pulses "will have the greatest damaging effect when the charge is detonated below the hull" (section XV).

Patterns, and what a log can say

The escorts dropped charges from the stern rack and fired them from throwers to either side, in patterns; "a proportionate number of charges with these maximum settings were usually included in each attack pattern" (3-12). BANG's report of 19 September 1944 gives the patterns as the boat heard them, "patterns of 3 6 or 9"; no Japanese record found gives the pattern a ship laid. A log can give the count, the time, the boat's depth and how the charges sounded and shook her; it cannot give where they went off. The Bureau quotes the boats' own words: "first a detonator click … and then bang!"; "a deep rumble increasing to the final boom"; "two pings on the hull which sounded as though two small nuts had been dropped" (section XV), and notes that the pistols had no mechanism to make a click. Damage says more than sound: a boat that came through undamaged was outside the lethal distance of every charge, and the damage she did take shows how near the nearest came.

In the records: the water column of BANG's counter-attack of 19 September 1944, and every engagement whose boat's depth log is transcribed; BANG's dives through every patrol.
