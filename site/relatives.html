Researching a relative

Most families start with a name, a serial number, and a unit or a ship. This kit follows units and ships, not people: find your relative's unit, and its own records say where it was, often day by day.

Start from what the family has

  • Discharge papers name the unit or ship. An Army discharge (WD AGO Form 53-55) gives the organization at discharge; a Navy Notice of Separation (NAVPERS 553) lists the ships and stations served on.
  • The serial number is the key to the Army's daily records: a morning report names a soldier, by serial number, on every day the soldier's status changed.
  • Letters and envelopes often carry an APO or FPO number, or a Navy number, which the units' records can date and place.

Your relative's own service record

Veterans and their next of kin can request a service record from the National Personnel Records Center. Many Army records were lost in the 1973 fire there: an estimated 80% of those for soldiers discharged between 1 November 1912 and 1 January 1960. When the personal file is gone, the unit's records are often the best that survive, and they are the ones this kit reads.

Then on the map

If the unit or ship is already in the kit, it's in the lists on the front page, by theater and date, and each mark on its map opens the page it was read from. If it isn't, share it as a lead (a free GitHub account is needed), or do it yourself with the kit's tools.

What stays with your family

The records here are the units', and they leave people out: no one's name, serial number, or own path through a hospital or a replacement depot goes into them. Keep your relative's story in your family's own notes, and use the kit's maps underneath it.
