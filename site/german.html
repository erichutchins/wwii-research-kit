German grids: the Kriegsmarine's naval squares and the Army's reference points

The German records rarely give a latitude or a map reference. The Naval Staff's diary puts a ship in a naval square, CJ 9472. The Army's reports from the Cassino front put a tank on the north edge of XXIII 89. Both are codes that only mean a place with the right chart or map in hand. These are being rebuilt from the charts and maps the Germans used, captured and filmed after the war, and tested on places the same records name.

The Kriegsmarine's naval grid

CJ 9472

The Naval Staff's war diary, 27 March 1944: "three destroyers heading north in CJ 9^72 at the northern coast of Sicily" (skl:55/p397; the translator's 9^72 read as 9472). CJ is the large square (Großquadrat), the central Mediterranean. 94 is a square printed large on the chart: block 9 of CJ, then its part 4. 72 splits that square twice more, down to a cell about 6 nautical miles across.

123456789 Every digit after the letters splits the square before it three by three, numbered like a telephone keypad: 1 north-west, 5 the centre, 9 south-east. A regular large square is 486 nautical miles on a side, so four digits come down to about 6 nm. A trailing 0 means the digit wasn't given. The grid is laid on a Mercator chart in whole minutes: in this part of the Mediterranean the smallest cell is 6′ of latitude by 8′ of longitude, and a block (the first digit) is 2°42′ by 3°36′.

In the open ocean the squares are regular and can be computed. In the Mediterranean they are not: CH and CJ were bent to follow the coasts, CJ's Sardinian column sits one row lower than the rest, and CN 11–13 don't exist. So each block here was measured or read off the wartime charts themselves (NARA microfilm T-1022, roll 2099: the U-boat section's Mediterranean operation charts, drawn on the OKM's charts 1890 G, 1891 G and 1892 G).

The anchors: diary squares with a place named

A square can be checked where the diary names a place beside it. "15 miles southeast of Syracuse" gives a point; "off Algier" gives only a side of the coast. The loose ones test the layout of the blocks, not their edges.

What isn't known yet

  • CH and CJ's outer edges. The blocks are placed; the large squares' stepped outlines along the coasts of Spain and Algeria are not drawn.
  • North of 45°36′N (BH, BJ, the top of the Adriatic) the cells are 10′ wide, not 8′: not in the decoder yet.
  • CK's rows rest on the lattice measured farther west and south, not on a latitude scale of their own.

The Army's reference points (Bezugspunkte)

On the Cassino and Garigliano front in 1944 the German Army's reports name few places. They give codes: "71 35", "2 km südwestl. 60 22", "61 t Panzer am Nordrand XXIII 89". These aren't grid references. They are numbered points on a secret overprint of the 1:100,000 map, issued so that a report sent by wire or radio gave nothing away without it.

What the map was

Geheim! / Italien 1 : 100 000 / Zusammendruck Gaeta / Maßstab 1:100000 (1 cm der Karte = 1 km der Natur) / Bezugspunktkarte 1:100000 / 4. Ausgabe / Achtung! Diese Karte darf nicht in die vordere Linie mitgenommen werden. / … / Bearbeitung u. Druck: Verm.u.Kart.Abt. (mot) 573
Secret! Italy 1:100,000, combined sheet Gaeta. Scale 1:100,000 (1 cm on the map = 1 km on the ground). Reference-point map 1:100,000, 4th edition. Caution! This map must not be taken into the front line. Prepared and printed by Survey and Mapping Battalion (motorized) 573.
The title block of Section 5, as printed: t312-0090/f0003 (NARA T-312 roll 90, Tenth Army's attachments for March 1944).

The map cuts the ground into irregular blocks, each 15–25 km across with a large block number, and inside each block draws small circles on towns, summits, crossroads and bridges, numbered from about 11 upward. The block numbers are scrambled on the ground: 61, 59 and 42 lie side by side. The warning is the point of it: the key stayed at the staffs, and a report could be read only there.

How a code reads

XXIII 68 = 8Z 57

The 5th Mountain Division's daily report of 14 February 1944, in the XIV Panzer Corps' files: "25 Pz. von ostw. XXIII 69 in Richtung Caira (XXIII 68)" (25 tanks from east of XXIII 69 toward Caira) (t314-0544/f0361). The February edition's blocks were printed as a digit and a letter. Tenth Army's situation maps carry a Roman numeral written by hand beside some of them, XXIII beside 8Z, and beside two others "+ 11": the reports gave the block by its cover name and added 11 to the point's printed number. So XXIII 68 is printed point 57 of block 8Z, the circle on Caira.

Offsets are measured from a point ("2 km südwestl. 60 22", "600 m NW XXIII 72"), and "Nordrand" is the north edge of the town at the point. The decoder moves a point by a distance along the eight compass directions; it reports an edge but doesn't move for it.

Every position here is provisional, good to about 0.4 km: the dashed ring around each point. The frames were fitted to the map's own printed kilometre grid (Gauss-Krüger, an inference from its margin figures), then tested on towns; the error left is in the 1940s base map and in matching its towns to modern ones. Each point links to the frame it was read on. An open circle is a number read with doubt.

Try one

A Roman numeral or a digit-and-letter block picks the February edition by itself; two-digit blocks are read on the edition chosen. The cover names other than XXIII are read on the maps but their blocks' points aren't read yet.

The anchors

Points whose place is printed on the map, and codes in the reports whose place is named or can be inferred. The error is between the decoded point and the place's modern position.
