About, method and limits

About, method and limits

What these pages are, which ships and units they cover and why those, how a value gets from a scanned page to a mark on a map, and what has not been done.

What this is

A reconstruction of where some ships and units of the Second World War were, day by day and sometimes minute by minute, from the records they kept at the time: US submarine war patrol reports, ships' war diaries and action reports, Army morning reports and unit histories, Navy muster rolls. Where the other side's record of the same event has been found (the Imperial Japanese Navy's war diaries and escort reports, German corps and army records, the Seekriegsleitung's diary), it is set beside the American one on one map and one clock. Where it has not been found, the page says so.

The name says what it does. Charting WWII History charts: it finds what the records say and puts it on maps and timelines. It does not set out to analyse or interpret the war, and its makers are not historians. Where a page does go beyond a record (a placement, a translation, an inference), it says so and shows what it rests on.

It is three things with three names. The WWII unit records are the transcriptions, as data. The WWII research kit is the tools and guides that find, read and check them. Charting WWII History is this site, which puts the two together. It is the work of one researcher and of Claude, an AI model made by Anthropic, not of an institution.

Made with Claude

Claude did the reading. Working in Claude Code under the rules below, it found the records in the archives' catalogs, read every scanned page, transcribed the values, translated the Japanese, German, French and Italian entries, worked out the conversions and placements (clock times between zones, grid references to latitude and longitude, the partly rebuilt grids), drew the inferences the pages label as inferences, and wrote the tools and these pages. The researcher chose what to follow, set the rules, reviewed the results and decided what to publish.

That is why the rules are what they are. A record speaks for itself: every transcribed value is cited to the page it was read from, and one click shows the scan beside it. A translation, a computed position, a ranking of explanations is the kit's own work, labelled as derived, inferred or assumed, and should be weighed as such. Trust the citation, and check the reading against it.

What it covers, and why these

The selection is not a sample of the war. It began with one submarine, USS BANG (SS-385), and grew along the lines of one family's research: the ships and units its members served in, the boats and ships that worked beside them, and the forces that met them. The records name units and ships only, never the people who prompted the choice. Coverage is therefore deep in a few places and thin in most:

How a value gets here

  • Read on the scan. Every value is read from the image of the page. Text recognition is used to find pages, never as a value.
  • As typed. Dates, times, zone letters and coordinates are recorded exactly as the page has them, with ? for an illegible character. A correction goes in a separate field beside the typed value, with its reason; the typed value is never overwritten.
  • Cited to the page. Every row carries the page it was read from, and every mark on these pages opens that page. A National Archives citation reads rg38-74802746/p05: Record Group 38, catalog item 74802746, the item's fifth page. Secondary works and websites are cited by page or address, and are labelled as secondary.
  • Checked by arithmetic. A track is tested for legs no ship could have run, a position for falling on land, an attack sheet against its own triangle. A failure is resolved on the scan, or left visible.

What the labels mean

LabelMeaning
reportedThe record states it: a typed position, a logged time.
derivedComputed from reported values by a stated rule: a clock time converted between zones, a grid reference converted to latitude and longitude, a distance between two tracks.
inferredA conclusion the kit draws from the records, with its reasons given and the alternatives named.
assumedSupplied to make a drawing possible where the record is silent: a straight line between two reported positions, a town's centre for a unit "near" it. Hatched or dashed on the maps.

Sources are labelled by kind as well: a unit's own record, intelligence, press, a post-war assessment, a recollection, a secondary work. Two tellings that go back to one source count once. Conclusions are given in words, ranked with the reasons for and against, never as a score. A unit's presence in an area or under a command is context: it does not mean the unit, still less any one man, took part.

What has not been done

  • No second reading. Claude read every scan, and its rows were checked by the arithmetic tests and by the researcher's review, not by an independent second reader, human or otherwise. Expect transcription errors, and please report them.
  • Translations are Claude's. Japanese, German, French and Italian entries are given in the original with an English translation beside them. No translator has checked them; the original is always shown so that one can.
  • The grids are partly rebuilt. The Japanese kana grid and the German reference systems are reconstructed from positions known some other way. Each explainer says which keys are established, which are probable and which are unknown, and how far a decoded position can be trusted.
  • Weather, seafloor and relief are modern. The weather is a reanalysis (ERA5), the depths and terrain are present-day surveys. They are context, labelled as such, and no ship's log.
  • Absence is not evidence. "No entry found" means the records searched, which each page names, held none. It does not mean nothing happened.

How to cite

Cite the original record for any value you use: each mark gives its archive, item and page. To cite the transcription or a page, give the repository and the version, which the foot of every page of the published site carries:

Eric Hutchins, with Claude (Anthropic), Charting WWII History (site, built with the WWII research kit from the WWII unit records), https://erichutchins.github.io/wwii-research-kit/ (version of [date at the foot of the page]).
